01 / LEADERSHIP

Leadership is not a title.
It is a record of usefulness.

Nansana does not need leadership that exists only in speeches, ceremonies or election seasons. It needs leadership that can be felt in the everyday life of the community — in the young person searching for a first opportunity, the trader trying to grow a small business, the family navigating a rapidly changing city, and the citizen who wants public institutions to work as they should.

For Idi Matovu, this is where leadership begins: with usefulness.

It means being close enough to understand the problems people actually face, but ambitious enough to see what those problems could become when addressed systematically. It means listening before prescribing, bringing people and institutions together, turning ideas into practical initiatives, and measuring leadership not simply by what was promised, but by what changed.

Nansana makes that philosophy urgent. It is part of a generation of African communities growing faster than the systems designed to serve them. Young people are entering the workforce. Businesses are being created from limited capital. New neighborhoods are expanding. Mobility, sanitation, skills, infrastructure and public services are under increasing pressure. Yet within those same pressures lies extraordinary energy: entrepreneurs building livelihoods, young people imagining different futures, families investing in their communities, and citizens asking more of government.

Idi’s vision is not to treat these realities as disconnected problems. It is to build a local leadership model capable of connecting them.

A young person should be able to move from education to skills, from skills to work, and from work to enterprise. A small business should be able to move from survival to stability and from stability to growth. A growing neighborhood should gain better infrastructure and services as it expands. And citizens should be able to see what their leaders are working on, what progress has been made and where government has fallen short.

That requires a different relationship between leadership and community: listen carefully, set priorities, convene the right people, act, measure progress and report back.

Listen carefully→Set priorities→Convene→Act→Measure→Report back

The ambition is larger than a collection of projects. It is to demonstrate what practical, accountable local leadership can look like in a rapidly urbanizing African community — beginning in Nansana, contributing to Uganda, and sharing lessons that may matter far beyond both.

Because ultimately, leadership should leave something behind: stronger people, stronger communities and institutions more capable than the ones it inherited.

02 / NANSANA

A local story with continental relevance.

Nansana is not only a constituency. It is a case study in the future of urban Africa.

A youthful population, dense enterprise, rapid urban growth and proximity to Kampala create extraordinary opportunity—but also intense pressure on mobility, jobs, housing, sanitation, infrastructure and public services.

The strategic question is simple: will growth make life better for ordinary people?

Rooted locally. Engaged globally.

The questions confronting Nansana are not only Nansana’s questions. They are among the defining questions of the Global South.

Across Africa, a young and rapidly growing population is colliding with a changing world of work. Cities are expanding faster than infrastructure can always follow. Technology is changing how people learn, trade, work and access government. Climate pressures increasingly affect food systems, migration, infrastructure and household livelihoods. Small and informal businesses remain the economic reality for millions of people, even as governments search for pathways toward higher productivity, better jobs and more resilient economies.

Uganda sits at the center of many of these transformations. Between 600,000 and 700,000 young Ugandans enter the labor market each year. Creating enough productive opportunities for them is not simply an employment challenge; it is a question about the kind of economy, cities and institutions Uganda is building for the next generation.

Urbanization adds another dimension. Uganda’s urban population is projected to more than double over the next two decades. Done well, that transformation can connect people to markets, jobs, education, infrastructure and opportunity. Done poorly, rapid growth can deepen congestion, informality, inadequate services and inequality.

Nansana is therefore more than the place from which Idi Matovu comes. It is a window into a much larger transformation.

The trader trying to turn a small enterprise into a sustainable business is part of Africa’s productivity challenge. The young graduate searching for a first opportunity is part of the continent’s employment challenge. The family navigating transport, housing, drainage and public services is living the reality of African urbanization. And the local government trying to respond with limited resources is confronting one of the central questions of twenty-first-century governance: how do institutions keep pace with the societies they serve?

These are local questions. But they are also global development questions.

That is where Idi’s international engagement should begin.

Not with prestige. With relevance.

The purpose of engaging internationally should not be to accumulate photographs from conferences or proximity to powerful institutions. It should be to connect local problems with global knowledge, partnerships, capital and ideas — and then bring something useful home.

A conversation about youth employment should eventually mean better pathways between skills and opportunity in Nansana. A conversation about urban development should help inform how growing communities think about mobility, markets, infrastructure and public space. A relationship with a university should create possibilities for research, training or exchange. Engagement with businesses and investors should help expose local entrepreneurs to markets, expertise and capital.

And the exchange should work in both directions.

Nansana should not appear in international conversations merely as a community waiting for solutions from elsewhere. Its entrepreneurs, young people, community organizations and public institutions are already adapting to rapid change. Their experiences can contribute to how the world understands informal enterprise, urbanization, youth opportunity and local governance in the Global South.

That is the international proposition behind Idi Matovu’s leadership: build credibility through work at home, use that credibility to participate in larger conversations, and turn those relationships back into opportunity at home.

Nansana can learn from the world. The world can also learn from Nansana.
